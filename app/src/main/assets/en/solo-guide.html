Strategy guide

First moves

Work the fullest lines first. Scan rows, columns, and boxes already holding six or more digits: the missing cells there shrink to one or two candidates, and forced digits turn up fast. Enter every certain digit before writing a single pencil mark. On Killer boards, start with the extreme cages — a two-cell cage totalling 3, 4, 16, or 17 can only ever be one pair of digits. On Jigsaw boards, scan by region instead of box: a region sprawled across two rows often pins a digit to one cell.

Key patterns

Keep pencil marks local. Mark up the line or box you are actively working on, not the whole grid; a board full of marks hides the patterns you are looking for. Two cells in a line holding the same two candidates — a naked pair — clears those digits from every other cell in the line. The reverse also pays: a digit that fits in only one cell of a line goes in even if that cell lists three candidates. On Killer boards, use the 45 rule: every row, column, and box sums to 45, so when cages cover all but one cell of a line, the leftover cell is whatever makes the total.

Common traps

Guessing is the big one. If you are picking between two digits with no evidence, stop and re-scan — there is almost always a line you have not squeezed yet. In Killer, forgetting that digits cannot repeat inside a cage, even where the cage wanders across two boxes, quietly poisons sums. In Jigsaw, the trap is habit: checking the middle "box" that does not exist. Work region by region.

Sources

  • Rules the guide leans on — each row/column/block holds one of each digit; Jigsaw replaces the blocks with arbitrary shapes; Killer cages carry a sum and no digit may repeat inside a cage, even across region boundaries: Simon Tatham, "Chapter 11: Solo", https://www.chiark.greenend.org.uk/~sgtatham/puzzles/doc/solo.html
  • "Work where the grid is fullest" and the named patterns — naked singles are easiest to spot in crowded regions; a naked pair (two cells in a unit holding the same two candidates) removes those digits from the unit's other cells; a hidden single is a digit with only one home in a unit: "Sudoku Strategy: Techniques from Singles to X-Wing and Chains", https://www.blinky.games/learn/sudoku-strategy.html
  • Killer specifics — the only two-cell sums with a single combination are 3 {1,2}, 4 {1,3}, 16 {7,9}, 17 {8,9}; every row, column and box sums to 45, which is what makes the leftover-cell deduction work: "Killer Sudoku Cage Combinations Chart", https://www.puzzlemakeronline.com/killer-sudoku-combinations/
